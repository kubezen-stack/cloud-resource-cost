# Experiment: naming the usage type behind a cost spike

**Question.** When the detector reports that a service cost more than expected, can the daily
notification say which part of the bill grew, and is the method used better than the obvious ones?

Reproduce: `python -m app.jobs.explain_benchmark --trials 500` (about a second, no database, network or API key).
Code: `app/services/anomaly_explain.py` (the method), `app/services/explain_benchmark.py` (this experiment),
tests in `tests/unit/test_anomaly_explain.py` and `tests/unit/test_explain_benchmark.py`.

## The method

For an anomaly on a day, Cost Explorer is queried once for that service, split by usage type and region,
for the day and the 14 days before it. For every usage type the expected cost is the **median of those 14
days** and the increase is the day's cost minus it. Usage types are listed largest increase first until they
cover 80% of the total increase (at most three). A usage type with no cost at all in the 14 days is marked
*new*. A small table recognises common usage types (instance hours, NAT Gateway data, data transfer, EBS,
requests, ...); an unknown type is shown as it is.

The notification then reads, for example:

> Amazon Virtual Private Cloud: `$162.0` instead of `$51.0` (+218%) — main driver: `EUC1-NatGateway-Bytes`
> (eu-central-1, data processed by a NAT Gateway) +$108.00 (100% of the increase)

It says **what** grew. It does not say why; that is for the person who knows what was deployed or launched.

## Method of the experiment

A synthetic service is built from six usage types, each with a level and a noise: a large steady instance
line ($120, 5% noise), two small NAT lines, a noisy data transfer line ($8, 25%), disks and requests. One
spike is injected into a known usage type, and each method has to name it first. 500 services per scenario,
fixed seeds.

| Scenario | Injected |
|---|---|
| one type costs 3x | a random line multiplied by 3 |
| one type gets +$50 | $50 added to a random line |
| a new usage type | a line that had no cost before appears at $50 |
| two types, +$80 and +$30 | the cause to name is the larger one |

Methods compared: the **biggest line of the bill** that day (what one sees looking at the bill), the **biggest
relative growth** over the median, and the **biggest increase over the median in dollars** (the method above).

## Results

Share of services where the injected usage type is named first:

| Method | one type costs 3x | one type gets +$50 | a new usage type | two types, +$80 and +$30 | Average |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| biggest line of the bill | 0.17 | 0.16 | 0.00 | 0.15 | 0.12 |
| biggest relative growth | **1.00** | 0.96 | 1.00 | 0.69 | 0.91 |
| **biggest increase in dollars (production)** | 0.85 | **1.00** | 1.00 | **1.00** | **0.96** |

## Reading the results

- **Looking at the bill does not work** (0.12): the biggest line is almost always the instances, which are
  not what changed. This is the case for the feature.
- **Neither of the other two wins everywhere.** Relative growth names a small line that tripled (1.00) but
  confuses a small noisy line with the real cause when two things change (0.69). The dollar increase finds the
  larger of two spikes and a fixed extra amount every time, and loses 15% of the "3x" cases.
- **Those 15% are mostly not mistakes about the money.** When a $3 line triples, it adds $6, and the noise of
  the $120 instance line (5%) is as large. Naming the instances as the main driver is then right in dollars and
  wrong against the injected cause. The method ranks by money, which is what a cost notification is for.
- **Ranking by dollars was chosen over ranking by growth** because the second one reports lines that jump
  from $0.10 to $0.40 ahead of the line that cost $30 more.

## Limits

- The services, levels and noise are generated by the author, so the scores show the behaviour of the methods
  on these shapes, not on real bills. A real service has many more usage types, some billed once a week.
- Only one day is explained and the baseline is the previous 14 days. A spike that lasts several days is
  explained on each day by itself, against a baseline that has started to include the spike.
- The usage type is the finest level used. Which resource, tag or deployment caused it is not known;
  that needs the opt-in resource-level data of Cost Explorer.
- The table of usage-type meanings covers common types and misses many; it is a convenience, not part of the score.
